# The wave-packet solution, derived — essential path only

This is the middle-length version: every load-bearing step from the model (Adams 2024, Eqs. 3.34–3.42) to the packet solution $P_{cv} = \bar P + \delta\cos\phi$, shown in full — and nothing that is not on that path. Cut relative to `expander_two_timescale_notes.md` (the full notes): the enthalpy identity and its cycle average, entropy remarks, the cross-checks (energy method, admittance equivalence, exact-root comparison), the third root, dropped-term ledgers, and validation detail. When you want any of those, the full notes have them; section numbers there are cited in brackets.

## 0. Strategy

The simulated signal is a fast oscillation whose amplitude and mean drift slowly — a wave packet. The plan, in four moves. **(i)** Split every variable into a cycle mean plus a first harmonic with slowly varying amplitude. **(ii)** On the fast timescale the valves barely act, so one cycle is a sealed gas oscillation — that fixes the carrier frequency $\omega(\bar P)$ (radio vocabulary, used throughout: the fast sinusoid itself is the carrier, its slowly varying height is the envelope — here the raw 100 to 130 Hz wiggle versus the slow growth and collapse of its amplitude). **(iii)** The small once-per-cycle valve response, projected onto the first harmonic, acts as gain or loss on the oscillation — that fixes the envelope rate $\lambda$. **(iv)** The cycle average of the equations fixes the drift of the mean $\bar P$. The output is three slow ODEs; the packet is rebuilt from their solution.

## 1. The model

CV with inlet/outlet reed valves; gas piston (mass $m$, face area $A$, damping $b$) between the CV and a sealed passive gas volume.

$$\frac{dM}{dt} = F_{in} - F_{out} \tag{1}$$

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = -P\frac{dV}{dt} + F_{in}c_pT_{in} - F_{out}c_pT \tag{2}$$

$$E = c_vMT, \qquad PV = MRT \tag{3}$$

$$m\ddot x + b\dot x = (P - P_{pass})A, \qquad V = V_{0c} + Ax \tag{4}$$

$$P_{pass}(x) = P_{p0}\left(\frac{V_{0p}}{V_{0p} - Ax}\right)^{\gamma} \tag{5}$$

$$F(\Delta P) = c_1\dot m_{max}(1-u)\sqrt{u} \quad \text{for } 0 \lt u \lt 1, \quad \text{else } 0; \qquad u = \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta P_{max}}, \quad c_1 = \frac{3\sqrt 3}{2} \tag{6}$$

with $F_{in} = F(P_{in}-P)$, $F_{out} = F(P-P_{out})$, plenum conditions $P_{in}, T_{in}, P_{out}$ fixed, and $R = c_p - c_v$, $\gamma = c_p/c_v$.

## 2. The model as one pressure equation

From (3), eliminating $MT = PV/R$: $E = \frac{c_v}{R}PV$. Differentiate and insert into (2):

$$\frac{c_v}{R}\dot PV + \frac{c_v}{R}P\dot V = -P\dot V + c_p(F_{in}T_{in} - F_{out}T)$$

Collect the $P\dot V$ terms using $1 + c_v/R = c_p/R$, then multiply by $R/(c_vV)$ using $(R/c_v)c_p = \gamma R$:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{\gamma R}{V}\left(F_{in}T_{in} - F_{out}T\right) - \gamma\frac{P}{V}\frac{dV}{dt} \tag{7}$$

This is the whole thermodynamics of the CV in one line: enthalpy fluxes in, boundary work out. Two limits used constantly below: valves shut gives $\dot P/P = -\gamma\dot V/V$, i.e.

$$PV^{\gamma} = \mathrm{const} \tag{8}$$

— the CV is a gas spring; and the fluctuating part of the flows will supply the drive.

## 3. The split

Write every variable as cycle mean plus first harmonic with slowly varying complex amplitude:

$$P(t) = \bar P(t) + P'(t), \qquad P' = \mathrm{Re}\left[\hat p(t)e^{i\phi(t)}\right], \qquad \dot\phi = \omega(\bar P), \qquad \delta = \lvert\hat p\rvert \tag{9}$$

and the same for $T, M, x, V$ ($a = \lvert\hat x\rvert$). The split is meaningful because of the slowness condition: the envelope and mean change by only a fraction $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-2}$ per radian — in the runs, growth rates are 2 to 4 per second and drift rates below 1 per second, against $\omega \approx 550$ to $820$ per second. One more number does a lot of work below: per cycle the valves exchange only $F/(Mf) \approx 0.3$ percent of the CV mass, so on the fast timescale the valve terms in (7) are small perturbations. The method is then mechanical: project each equation onto its cycle mean (section 7) and onto its first harmonic (sections 4–6). Harmonics $2\phi, 3\phi$ generated by the valve nonlinearity drive the resonator off-resonance and stay at order $\varepsilon$ — dropped. (There is no $e^{-ikx}$ factor: at 130 Hz the wavelength is ~2.8 m against a 0.15 m device, so pressure is uniform in each volume and the neck gas moves as a slug — full notes 3.3.)

## 4. The carrier: one cycle is a sealed gas oscillation

To leading order the fast motion obeys the sealed relation (8). Linearize it about the cycle mean — expand $(\bar P + P')(\bar V + V')^\gamma$ to first order — and use $V' = Ax'$:

$$\frac{P'}{\bar P} + \gamma\frac{V'}{\bar V} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P' = -\frac{k_{cv}}{A}x', \qquad k_{cv} = \frac{\gamma\bar PA^2}{\bar V_{cv}} \tag{10}$$

The CV pushes back on the piston like a spring of constant $k_{cv}$ (force $= AP' = -k_{cv}x'$). Now name the amplitudes: just as $\delta = \lvert\hat p\rvert$ is the pressure amplitude, $a = \lvert\hat x\rvert$ is the amplitude of the piston's displacement oscillation, in meters (millimeters in practice) — the mechanical counterpart of $\delta$. Taking magnitudes of (10) converts one into the other:

$$\delta = \frac{k_{cv}a}{A} \tag{11}$$

The conversion matters because the oscillator equation — and hence the envelope law of section 6 — is naturally written for the piston variable $a$ (the piston is what carries mass, springs, and damping), while a pressure trace measures $\delta$; (11) is the dictionary between them, used silently whenever both appear.

Linearizing the passive adiabat (5) the same way gives the second spring, $k_{pass} = \gamma\bar P_{pass}A^2/\bar V_{pass}$. The piston between two gas springs oscillates at

$$\omega(\bar P) = \sqrt{\frac{k_{cv}+k_{pass}}{m}} = A\sqrt{\frac{\gamma\bar P}{m}\left(\frac{1}{\bar V_{cv}} + \frac{1}{\bar V_{pass}}\right)} \tag{12}$$

(second form uses $\bar P_{pass} = \bar P$, from the quasi-static piston of section 7). That is the carrier, chirping as $\sqrt{\bar P}$, and $\phi(t) = \phi_0 + \int_0^t\omega~dt'$. The temperature rides along on the sealed isentrope: $T'/\bar T = \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}P'/\bar P$, in phase with $P'$.

## 5. The valve feedback

### 5.1 Small amplitude: the tangent slope

Keep the order-$\varepsilon$ valve terms in the fast part of (7). Expand the flows about the mean pressure differences $\Delta\bar P_{in} = P_{in} - \bar P$ and $\Delta\bar P_{out} = \bar P - P_{out}$:

$$F_{in} \approx \bar F_{in} - F'_{in}P', \qquad F_{out} \approx \bar F_{out} + F'_{out}P' \tag{13}$$

(opposite signs: raising $P$ starves the inlet, feeds the outlet; $F' = dF/d\Delta P$). Now substitute everything into (7): $P = \bar P + P'$, $T = \bar T + T'$, $V = \bar V_{cv} + Ax'$, $\dot V = d\bar V_{cv}/dt + A\dot x'$, the flows (13), and the prefactor $1/V = (1/\bar V_{cv})(1 - Ax'/\bar V_{cv} + \dots)$. Multiplying out to first order in the fluctuations, every term lands in one of three bins. Purely slow, no oscillating factor:

$$\frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}\bar F_{in} - \bar T\bar F_{out}\right) - \frac{\gamma\bar P}{\bar V_{cv}}\frac{d\bar V_{cv}}{dt}$$

Oscillating, linear in one fluctuation:

$$-\frac{\gamma\bar PA}{\bar V_{cv}}\dot x' - \frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}F'_{in} + \bar TF'_{out}\right)P' + \text{three small terms}$$

(the small terms — the $T'$ feedback on the outflow enthalpy, the $x'$ modulation of the prefactor, and slow-rate coefficients multiplying $P'$ — are each about one percent of the terms kept; sized in full notes 4.2). Quadratic in two fluctuations, for example $\gamma AP'\dot x'/\bar V_{cv}$: a product of two first harmonics contains only a constant part and a second harmonic, nothing at the carrier, so it contributes nothing here — its constant part is saved for section 7. The left side splits the same way, $dP/dt = d\bar P/dt + dP'/dt$.

Now the projection. The substituted equation holds at every instant, and a slowly varying function cannot cancel a function oscillating at the carrier — over any cycle the two are orthogonal (average the equation: the oscillating bin dies; multiply by $\cos\phi$ and average: the slow bin dies). So the slow parts and the oscillating parts must balance separately — one substitution, two equations. The slow balance,

$$\frac{d\bar P}{dt} = \frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}\bar F_{in} - \bar T\bar F_{out}\right) - \frac{\gamma\bar P}{\bar V_{cv}}\frac{d\bar V_{cv}}{dt} + \text{quadratic means}$$

is set aside for section 7, which upgrades it in two ways that only make sense after 5.2: the bare $\bar F$ become the rectified $F_0$, and the quadratic means become the acoustic power. The oscillating balance is this section's result — with $\gamma\bar PA/\bar V_{cv} = k_{cv}/A$ from (10),

$$\frac{dP'}{dt} = -\frac{k_{cv}}{A}\frac{dx'}{dt} + gP', \qquad g = -\frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}F'_{in} + \bar TF'_{out}\right) \tag{14}$$

The first term is the gas spring of (10); the second is new — the valves feeding pressure fluctuation back on itself, at rate $g$. Its sign comes from the curve slope,

$$F'(\Delta P) = \frac{c_1\dot m_{max}}{\Delta P_{max}}\cdot\frac{1 - 3u}{2\sqrt u} \tag{15}$$

negative on the falling branch $\frac13 \lt u \lt 1$ (valve pumps, $g \gt 0$), positive on the rising branch (valve damps), zero when closed.

### 5.2 Finite amplitude: the first-harmonic gain

What this subsection is for: the fast equation (14) needs, for each valve, a single number — how strongly the flow responds at the oscillation frequency, per unit of pressure swing. That number multiplies $P'$ and becomes the gain $g$ that will set the envelope's growth rate. Section 5.1 took it to be the slope $F'$ at the mean, which is correct only while the swing is small enough that the curve looks straight across it. In the actual runs it is not: the swing $\delta$ is comparable to the full width $\Delta P_{max}$ of the curve, so over one cycle the valve's operating point travels across curved regions, through the flow maximum, and past the closing point — the valve spends part of every cycle completely shut, and its flow waveform looks nothing like slope-times-sinusoid. In some biases the tangent fails outright: when the mean difference exceeds the closing value, $\Delta\bar P \gt \Delta P_{max}$, the valve is closed at the mean, $F$ and $F'$ are both zero there, and (14) with (15) predicts a dead valve while the simulation plainly oscillates.

The repair rests on two facts. First, the valve is memoryless — its instantaneous flow is a fixed function of its instantaneous $\Delta P$ — so, driven by $\Delta P(t) = \Delta\bar P + \delta\cos\psi$, its output is a periodic, even function of $\psi$, hence a cosine series. Second, of that series only two pieces matter: the mean (the slow equations of section 7 use nothing but cycle averages) and the first harmonic (the resonator responds strongly only at its own frequency; the $2\psi$, $3\psi$ components drive it off-resonance and are suppressed by order $\varepsilon$). So keep exactly those two:

$$F_0(\Delta\bar P, \delta) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}F(\Delta\bar P + \delta\cos\psi)d\psi, \qquad N = \frac{1}{\pi\delta}\int_0^{2\pi}F(\Delta\bar P + \delta\cos\psi)\cos\psi~d\psi \tag{16}$$

The two coefficients have concrete physical readings — they are the two measurements an experimentalist would take on the driven valve. $F_0$ (units kg/s) is the DC flow-meter reading: the net mass the valve transports per cycle divided by the period — the only part of the flow that changes how much gas the CV stores, hence the only part the slow equations feel. It is generally not $F(\Delta\bar P)$: a valve biased shut at the mean still shows $F_0 \gt 0$ if the swing opens it briefly each cycle, the way a diode rectifies AC into DC. $N$ (units kg/s per Pa) is the lock-in reading referenced to the carrier: the component of the flow waveform oscillating in sync with the drive, per unit drive — the valve's effective conductance at the oscillation frequency, measured with the actual finite swing rather than an infinitesimal probe. Only this synchronized component can exchange net energy with the resonance, hence only $N$ enters the gain. Its sign is the physics: $N \gt 0$ is resistor-like (flow up when $\Delta P$ up — absorbs oscillation energy), $N \lt 0$ means the flow arrives preferentially when $\Delta P$ is low — negative resistance, the push landing in the amplifying phase. (In acoustic terms $N$ is the reed admittance of thesis Eq. 3.31, in mass-flow units, generalized to finite amplitude.)

$N$ is the first-harmonic gain — output at the oscillation frequency per unit input at the oscillation frequency — which is precisely the role $F'$ played in (13). So (14) holds verbatim with

$$g(\bar P, \delta) = -\frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}N_{in} + \bar TN_{out}\right) \tag{17}$$

As $\delta \to 0$, Taylor expansion under the integrals gives $N \to F'$, so (17) contains section 5.1 as its small-amplitude limit. At finite $\delta$ the two integrals quietly do everything the tangent could not. A valve closed at its mean still has $F_0 \neq 0$: it opens during the part of the cycle when the swing carries it into its open band, and the resulting net rectified inflow is what charges the mean. The same valve has $N \neq 0$: its brief opening near one extreme of the swing responds coherently at the oscillation frequency, and that is what pumps the envelope. In the worked case of `describing_function_illustration.png` the opening occurs where the valve's own $\Delta P$ is smallest — most flow when least pressure difference — so $N \lt 0$, an effective negative resistance produced purely by the finite amplitude.

Why keep section 5.1 if this one supersedes it? Three reasons. Structure: 5.1 is where the fast equation (14) is derived at all — where "valve action acts as a gain on $P'$" is established; 5.2 changes only the number inside it. Transparency: $F'$ is a closed formula, (15), whose sign is read directly off the curve, and the limit $N \to F'$ — the check that the general theory contains the simple one — cannot even be stated without it. Onset: whether an infinitesimal disturbance grows from rest is exactly a small-amplitude question, so 5.1 answers the self-start question exactly; in your notebook's bias its answer is that the machine does not self-start ($F' = 0$ at the mean), which is why the simulation begins with a finite velocity kick — and from that finite amplitude onward, 5.2 governs.

## 6. The envelope law

### 6.1 The fast system and its characteristic cubic

The fast dynamics are now two equations — (14) for the pressure fluctuation, and the fluctuating part of the piston equation (4), with the passive spring linearized:

$$\frac{dP'}{dt} = -\frac{k_{cv}}{A}\dot x' + gP', \qquad m\ddot x' + b\dot x' + k_{pass}x' = AP' \tag{18}$$

Eliminate $P'$: differentiate the piston equation once, substitute $\dot P'$ from the first, then use the piston equation again to replace $AP'$. Writing $k = k_{cv}+k_{pass}$:

$$m\dddot x' + (b - gm)\ddot x' + (k - gb)\dot x' - gk_{pass}x' = 0$$

so $x' \propto e^{st}$ requires

$$ms^3 + (b - gm)s^2 + (k - gb)s - gk_{pass} = 0 \tag{19}$$

### 6.2 Solving the cubic perturbatively

With $b = g = 0$, (19) is $s(ms^2 + k) = 0$: exact roots $0$ and $\pm i\omega_0$, $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ — the undamped oscillator. The perturbations are tiny on the oscillator scale, and each for its own physical reason. $b/m = \omega_0/Q$ is small because the resonator is a good one ($Q \approx 60$ to $240$: it rings for many cycles). $g$ is small because the valves are tiny orifices feeding a large reservoir: from (14) with the gain scale $N \sim \dot m_{max}/\Delta P_{max}$ and $\gamma R\bar T/\bar V_{cv} = \gamma\bar P/\bar M$,

$$\frac{g}{\omega} \sim \gamma\cdot\frac{\bar P}{\Delta P_{max}}\cdot\frac{\dot m_{max}}{\bar M\omega} \approx 1.4\times12\times10^{-3} \approx 0.02$$

— the valve passes only about $10^{-3}$ of the CV mass per radian, leveraged twelve-fold because flow responds on the narrow $\Delta P_{max}$ scale rather than the $\bar P$ scale. (A valve large enough to defeat this would exchange an order-one gas fraction per radian, and the device would be a flow channel, not a resonator.) $\lambda$ then inherits smallness as a difference of the other two. Numbers: $g \approx 25$, $b/m \approx 3$, $\lambda \approx 2$ to $4$, all per second, against $\omega_0$ of $550$ to $820$. Simple roots move smoothly with coefficients, so write the root near $+i\omega_0$ as

$$s = i\omega_0 + \lambda, \qquad \lambda \text{ small and a priori complex}$$

Binomials with $i^2 = -1$, $i^3 = -i$, dropping quadratic-in-small terms: $s^2 = -\omega_0^2 + 2i\omega_0\lambda$ and $s^3 = -i\omega_0^3 - 3\omega_0^2\lambda$. Substitute into (19) and discard all products of two small quantities ($gb$, $\lambda b$, $\lambda g$, $\lambda^2$):

$$\left[ik\omega_0 - im\omega_0^3\right] + \left[-3m\omega_0^2\lambda + k\lambda - (b - gm)\omega_0^2 - gk_{pass}\right] = 0$$

One complex equation is two real equations. The imaginary part is $\omega_0(k - m\omega_0^2)$ plus $(k-3m\omega_0^2)$ times the imaginary part of $\lambda$; the first piece vanishes by the choice $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ (which is what defines the carrier — any other center would force a large shift), and what remains forces $\mathrm{Im}\lambda = 0$: no frequency shift at first order, (12) stands. The real part, with $k = m\omega_0^2$ so that $-3m\omega_0^2\lambda + k\lambda = -2m\omega_0^2\lambda$:

$$-2m\omega_0^2\lambda = (b - gm)\omega_0^2 + gk_{pass}$$

$$\lambda = -\frac{b}{2m} + \frac{g}{2}\cdot\frac{k_{cv}}{k_{cv}+k_{pass}} \tag{20}$$

(the last step uses $m\omega_0^2 = k$ to combine $\frac g2 - \frac g2\frac{k_{pass}}{k} = \frac g2\frac{k_{cv}}{k}$). With $b = m\omega_0/Q$ the loss term is $\omega_0/2Q$. At finite amplitude $g = g(\bar P, \delta)$ from (17) — the formula survives because its derivation used only linearity in the first harmonics, which (16) preserves by construction.

### 6.3 From growth rate to envelope

Two independent facts combine into the envelope law. First, an eigenvalue is an amplitude law: the root $s = i\omega_0 + \lambda$ means the fast solution behaves as $x' \propto e^{st} = e^{\lambda t}e^{i\omega_0 t}$ — a phase rotating at $\omega_0$ times a magnitude $e^{\lambda t}$ — and the magnitude of the oscillation is exactly what $a$ names. So at frozen slow state, $\dot a = \lambda a$; under slow drift, $\lambda(\bar P, \delta)$ at the instantaneous state applies, with error of order $\varepsilon^2$ since the coefficients barely change within a cycle.

Second, even with $\lambda = 0$ a drifting frequency changes the amplitude. Take the bare oscillator with slowly varying stiffness, $\ddot x' + \omega^2(t)x' = 0$, energy $E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot x'^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x'^2$. Differentiate and use the equation of motion — the dynamical terms cancel and only the explicit drift of the stiffness survives:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = m\dot x'\left(\ddot x' + \omega^2x'\right) + m\omega\dot\omega x'^2 = m\omega\dot\omega x'^2$$

Cycle-average with $\langle x'^2\rangle = a^2/2$ and $E = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2a^2$:

$$\left\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\right\rangle = \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega}E \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{E}{\omega}\right) = \frac{\dot E}{\omega} - \frac{E\dot\omega}{\omega^2} = 0$$

so $E/\omega$ is conserved under slow drift — the adiabatic invariant. With $E/\omega = \frac{1}{2}m\omega a^2$ constant, $a \propto \omega^{-1/2}$, i.e. $\dot a/a = -\dot\omega/2\omega$: as the gas springs stiffen with the rising mean, the same oscillation energy fits into a smaller swing. Both effects are first order in $\varepsilon$, so they superpose (cross-terms are $\varepsilon^2$; for scale, $\dot\omega/2\omega \sim 0.1$ per second against $\lambda$ of 2 to 4):

$$\frac{da}{dt} = \left[\lambda(\bar P, \delta) - \frac{\dot\omega}{2\omega}\right]a \tag{21}$$

This is linear in $a$, so it integrates exactly: $a(t) = a(0)\exp\int\left[\lambda - \dot\omega/2\omega\right]dt'$. The second piece is a perfect derivative ($\dot\omega/\omega = d\ln\omega/dt$), so its integral is $\frac12\ln(\omega(t)/\omega(0))$ regardless of the path, and exponentiating gives a square root. Converting to pressure amplitude with (11), whose coefficient $k_{cv}$ itself drifts:

$$\delta(t) = \delta(0)\cdot\frac{k_{cv}(t)}{k_{cv}(0)}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\omega(0)}{\omega(t)}}\cdot\exp\left(\int_0^t\lambda(\bar P,\delta)dt'\right) \tag{22}$$

Growth while $\lambda \gt 0$, decay while $\lambda \lt 0$; and because $N$ depends on $\delta$, the condition $\lambda(\bar P, \delta) = 0$ is solvable for $\delta$ — the envelope self-limits, which is where limit cycles come from.

## 7. The packet center

The fast problem is done. What remains is the slow drift of the mean state, and the operation is the mirror image of sections 4 to 6: instead of keeping the parts of each equation that oscillate at the carrier, keep their cycle averages, term by term. Two averaging facts do all the work below: the average of a lone oscillating factor is zero, and the average of a product of two oscillations keeps only their in-phase combination, worth one half of amplitude times amplitude.

### 7.1 Mass

Average equation (1). Over one cycle the inflow waveform averages, by the definition (16), to exactly $F_{0,in}$ — likewise the outlet:

$$\frac{d\bar M}{dt} = F_{0,in} - F_{0,out} \tag{23}$$

Less trivial than it looks: these are the rectified flows, which can differ completely from the flow at the mean pressure — a valve closed at its mean still passes net gas whenever the swing opens it for part of each cycle. That rectified trickle is what charges the mean.

### 7.2 The flux term of the pressure equation

Average the first term of (7). Replace $1/V$ by $1/\bar V_{cv}$ and $T$ by $\bar T$ where they multiply the flows — their fluctuating parts enter only through products with the flow fluctuation, percent-level corrections (sized in full notes 6.4) — and average the flows as in 7.1:

$$\left\langle\frac{\gamma R}{V}\left(F_{in}T_{in} - F_{out}T\right)\right\rangle = \frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}F_{0,in} - \bar TF_{0,out}\right)$$

### 7.3 The work term: displacement work plus acoustic power

Write $P = \bar P + P'$ and $\dot V = d\bar V_{cv}/dt + \dot V'$ and multiply out. Four products: the two cross terms each contain one lone oscillating factor and average to zero; what survives is

$$\left\langle P\dot V\right\rangle = \bar P\frac{d\bar V_{cv}}{dt} + \left\langle P'\dot V'\right\rangle$$

— work pushing the slowly drifting mean piston position, plus the net work per cycle exchanged with the oscillation itself (the acoustic power).

The acoustic power needs one short computation. If the pressure fluctuation were purely the gas-spring response (10), $P' \propto -x'$, it would sit a quarter cycle out of phase with the velocity $\dot x'$ — velocity is the derivative of displacement, and differentiating a sinusoid shifts its phase by a quarter cycle ($x' \propto \cos\phi$ gives $\dot x' \propto -\sin\phi$); physically, spring pressure peaks at the turning points where the piston is momentarily at rest, and vanishes at mid-stroke where the piston is fastest. The in-phase rule above then gives $\langle P'\dot x'\rangle \propto \langle\cos\phi\sin\phi\rangle = 0$: a pure spring stores work on compression and returns all of it on expansion — lossless by phase, like a capacitor whose current and voltage sit ninety degrees apart. So the entire effect comes from the small valve correction. Solve the fast pressure equation (14) for the complex amplitudes (substitute $P' = \mathrm{Re}[\hat pe^{i\phi}]$, $x' = \mathrm{Re}[\hat xe^{i\phi}]$, so $d/dt \to i\omega$):

$$i\omega\hat p = -\frac{k_{cv}}{A}i\omega\hat x + g\hat p \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat p = -\frac{k_{cv}}{A}\hat x + i\frac{gk_{cv}}{\omega A}\hat x + O(g^2/\omega^2)$$

The first piece is the spring — in phase with $x'$, no net work. The second carries a factor $i$: shifted a quarter cycle, hence in phase with the velocity — this is the piece that works. With the product rule $\langle q_1q_2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}[\hat q_1\hat q_2^*]$ and velocity amplitude $i\omega\hat x$:

$$\left\langle P'\dot V'\right\rangle = A\left\langle P'\dot x'\right\rangle = \frac{gk_{cv}}{2}a^2 = \frac{g\bar V_{cv}}{2\gamma\bar P}\delta^2 \tag{24}$$

(conversion by (11) and the definition of $k_{cv}$). Sign reading: when $g \gt 0$ the mean state loses pressure paying for the growing oscillation; during decay, $g \lt 0$, the oscillation pays it back.

### 7.4 The mean piston: quasi-static balance

Average the piston equation (4): $m \cdot d^2\bar x/dt^2 + b \cdot d\bar x/dt = A(\bar P - \bar P_{pass})$. The left side involves only slow rates — smaller than the pressure-force scale by order $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon^2$ — so to leading order the mean piston position sits, at every instant, where the two mean pressures balance:

$$\bar P_{pass}(\bar x) = \bar P$$

As the CV charges, the piston slides toward the passive side, compressing it until its pressure catches up (this drifting equilibrium is what a rolling-median baseline tracks). Because the passive side is the sealed adiabat (5), the balance inverts in closed form:

$$\bar V_{pass} = V_{0p}\left(\frac{P_{p0}}{\bar P}\right)^{1/\gamma}, \qquad \bar V_{cv} = V_{0c} + V_{0p} - \bar V_{pass}, \qquad \frac{d\bar V_{cv}}{d\bar P} = \frac{\bar V_{pass}}{\gamma\bar P} \tag{25}$$

The middle relation holds because the total geometric volume $V_{0c} + V_{0p}$ is fixed: whatever the passive side loses, the CV gains. The derivative is kept because 7.5 needs it.

### 7.5 Assemble

Insert 7.2 and 7.3 into the average of (7):

$$\frac{d\bar P}{dt} = \frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}F_{0,in} - \bar TF_{0,out}\right) - \frac{\gamma\bar P}{\bar V_{cv}}\frac{d\bar V_{cv}}{dt} - \frac{\gamma}{\bar V_{cv}}\left\langle P'\dot V'\right\rangle$$

The middle term is not independent of the left side: by the chain rule and (25), $d\bar V_{cv}/dt = (\bar V_{pass}/\gamma\bar P)(d\bar P/dt)$, so that term equals $(\bar V_{pass}/\bar V_{cv})(d\bar P/dt)$. Move it across and factor:

$$\frac{d\bar P}{dt}\left(1 + \frac{\bar V_{pass}}{\bar V_{cv}}\right) = \frac{\gamma R}{\bar V_{cv}}\left(T_{in}F_{0,in} - \bar TF_{0,out}\right) - \frac{g\delta^2}{2\bar P}$$

(the last term is (24) times $\gamma/\bar V_{cv}$). Multiply through by $\bar V_{cv}$ and divide:

$$\frac{d\bar P}{dt} = \frac{\gamma R\left(T_{in}F_{0,in} - \bar TF_{0,out}\right) - \bar V_{cv}g\delta^2/2\bar P}{\bar V_{cv} + \bar V_{pass}} \tag{26}$$

Note that both volumes in (26) are not additional unknowns: they are evaluated from (25) at the current $\bar P$ — algebraic functions of the mean pressure, recomputed at every instant of the integration (the passive volume from the balance-and-adiabat inversion, the CV volume as the remainder of the fixed total $V_{0c} + V_{0p}$). The effective volume is the sum $\bar V_{cv} + \bar V_{pass}$ because the quasi-static piston transmits pressure: charging the CV inflates both springs, so the mean rises at roughly half the rate a rigid CV would (for equal volumes). The set closes with the mean gas law $\bar T = \bar P\bar V_{cv}/\bar MR$.

## 8. The packet, assembled

Three slow ODEs — (23) for $\bar M$, (26) for $\bar P$, (21) for $a$ — with the algebra (25), (12), (20), (17), (16), and $\delta = k_{cv}a/A$. Then

$$P_{cv}(t) = \bar P(t) + \delta(t)\cos\left(\phi_0 + \int_0^t\omega(\bar P)dt'\right) \tag{27}$$

with $T$ and $x$ slaved by section 4. The life of the packet is read off $\lambda$ and the drift: ignition while $\lambda \gt 0$; a limit cycle where the trajectory settles on the locus $\lambda(\bar P, \delta) = 0$ (your notebook's $V_{0,cv} = 1\times10^{-4}$ case — amplitude predicted 0.2947 bar, simulated 0.2946); turnover and collapse when the charging mean drags the valves out of their pumping band; a parabolic landing $P_{in} - \bar P \propto (t^\ast - t)^2$ once only the inlet trickles (integrate (26) with $F_0 \propto \sqrt{P_{in}-\bar P}$, substitute $y = \sqrt{P_{in}-\bar P}$); and ring-down $\delta \propto e^{-\omega t/2Q}$ when the valves disengage. Against full simulations, thesis parameters and your notebook's variant alike: mean within 0.003 bar, envelope 1 to 3 percent, frequency within 0.25 Hz.
